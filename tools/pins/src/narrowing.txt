01 Why Your AI Answers Keep Coming Back Generic
There's a particular kind of AI answer almost everyone has gotten at some point: technically fine,
nothing wrong with it exactly, and somehow still not useful. The kind of answer that could've been
handed to literally anyone who asked the same question, because that's exactly what happened.

What's going on has nothing to do with the AI being bad at its job.

Picture calling a doctor's office and telling the nurse, "I don't feel well." That's all you give them.
What happens? You get something generic: drink water, get some rest, come in if it doesn't improve
in a few days. Not wrong, necessarily. Just not useful, because there isn't enough there to be useful
with. The nurse doesn't know where it hurts, since when, or what you've already tried.

Now picture describing it properly: where it hurts, how long it's been going on, what makes it
worse, what you've already taken for it. Suddenly you're getting something specific, because the
advice is only as good as what it was built from.


AI behaves exactly like that first phone call, by default. It is capable. Most
people just hand it the one-line version and expect the detailed answer back.


Type "write me a workout plan" into an AI tool, and it has nothing to work with except those four
words. It doesn't know your fitness level, your schedule, your equipment, or what you're trying to
achieve, so it hands back something generic enough to apply to anyone. Adding "please be specific"
to that same question won't fix it. The problem is the missing information, not your tone.

02 The Fix: Narrow Before You Ask
The fix is to treat the whole exchange like a conversation that gets narrower every time you speak:
you go broad on purpose first, then use what comes back to ask something sharper, then sharper
again.

This is how it played out in a real case, step by step, with the actual prompts used.

The first prompt wasn't "what programming language should I learn." It was: "Research the most
common paths beginners take into programming right now, and what each one is useful for." Still
broad, on purpose. Its only job was to produce a real, researched shortlist to work from, not a final
answer. It came back with half a dozen real paths, each with a short explanation of what it's typically
used for.

The second prompt took that exact list and narrowed it: "Given that I have about five hours a week,
no technical background, and I want to eventually build small tools for myself rather than work as a
developer, which of these would you recommend, and why?" A completely different kind of question.
The AI isn't guessing anymore. It's working from material it already gathered, and being asked to
weigh it against real constraints, not invent an answer from nothing.

That two-step process is what produced something usable: one specific path, chosen for a reason
that holds up, instead of a generic "learn Python, it's beginner-friendly" answer that ignores the five
hours a week and the actual goal entirely.

Notice what each step did that the other couldn't. The first prompt's whole job was to generate real
material. The second prompt's whole job was to narrow that material using details only you could
supply. Skip the first step, and there's nothing real for the second step to narrow. Skip the second,
and you're left with a broad list and no way to tell which item on it is yours to pick.

03 The Three-Prompt Template
That same structure, turned into a template you can reuse for anything, not just business ideas. Copy
these three prompts, fill in your own situation, and run them in order, one at a time, waiting for each
answer before sending the next. Turn on web search, or the tool's deep research mode, before you
start: the word "research" in these prompts only means research if the tool can look things up. With
search off, it answers from memory.


     PROMPT ONE: GO BROAD, ON PURPOSE

     "Research realistic ways to [your goal]. Give me a shortlist, with a short explanation of
     each."


This prompt's only job is to generate real material. Don't expect, or ask for, a final answer yet.



     PROMPT TWO: NARROW USING YOUR REAL CONSTRAINTS

     "Research each of these in more depth, and tell me which two or three are the most
     realistic for someone with [your real constraint: time, budget, experience, equipment,
     etc.]."


This is where you hand over the specific details a stranger wouldn't know, the way a knowledgeable friend
would ask for them.



     PROMPT THREE: NARROW TO ONE, SPECIFIC CHOICE

     "Of these, which one specifically should I start with, and why, based on everything you just
     found?"


By now the AI is reasoning from real, researched material instead of guessing from a one-line question.

04 One Piece of a Larger Method
The rule underneath all three prompts, in one sentence: if answering your question well would
require someone to already know two or three things about your specific situation, don't hand AI
that question in one shot. Go broad first, then narrow using what it gives you back.

That rule solves exactly one problem: finding the right idea or direction instead of a generic one. It
doesn't solve the next problem: turning that idea into something built correctly, with zero guesswork
left for the AI to fill in on its own. That's a different skill, with its own method, and it's usually
where things quietly go wrong even after the idea itself was good.

That's also where most people stop improving. They learn to ask better questions, get a better idea
out of it, and assume the rest (building the thing, picking the right tool for it, catching it when the AI
is confidently wrong) will sort itself out. It doesn't. That's why someone can ask a great question and
still end up with a mediocre result.

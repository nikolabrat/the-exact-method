The Video Brief
Tell AI exactly how to make your video, then check what it
gives you. The setup, the brief, the checks and the skills
that keep AI video from looking AI-made.




A free, standalone guide. Set it up once, and every video after that starts from a script, voice files and one written brief.
01 What This File Does
This guide shows how to make short vertical videos (Reels, Shorts, TikToks) by giving Claude a
script, voice files and a written brief. Claude builds the video in code, renders it, checks it and sends
you the file. You never open an editing app.

You don't need to know how to code. Claude writes and runs it. Your job is to describe the video
precisely and judge the result. That's the work that makes it your video and not a template.

The guide has five parts: the setup (sections 02 to 05), the brief and the prompts (06 to 09), the
checks (10), the skills and plugins that keep the result from looking AI-made (11 to 14), and two
outside sources: reference videos to borrow motion from, and AI footage made from inside Claude
(15 and 16).


How to use it, in three steps: do the setup once, fill in the brief for each video, then send
the three prompts in order and review what comes back.

03 The Voice: License and Cost
ElevenLabs' free plan has no commercial license. Their help center says it directly: "The free plan
does not include a commercial license and cannot be used for any commercial purpose." Audio from
the free plan that you publish also has to credit ElevenLabs.

If your videos promote anything you sell (a product, a service, your own business), that's
commercial use, so you need a paid plan.

In practice it costs very little. The Starter plan is $6 a month, includes the commercial license and
comes with 30,000 credits. On the Eleven v4 model one character of script costs one credit, and a
45-second Reel script runs about 600 to 1,000 characters. That makes 30,000 credits enough for
more than 30 Reels a month.

04 Connecting ElevenLabs to Claude
Claude makes the voice files through an API key. A restricted key can only do what you allow and
only spend what you allow, so set it up this way.


Create the key

  1. Go to elevenlabs.io/app/developers/api-keys and click Create API key.

  2. Name it, for example claude-voiceovers.
  3. Turn on Restrict Key.

  4. Set a credit limit below your plan's total, for example 20,000 on Starter. The rest stays free for
     anything you make by hand.
  5. Under Endpoints, set Text to Speech to Access, Voices to Read and Models to Access. Leave
     every other endpoint on No Access.

  6. Leave Restrict by IP address off. Cloud workspaces change their address, and the key would
     stop working.

  7. Keep Auto-disable if leaked on.

  8. Click Create Key and copy the key. ElevenLabs shows it only once.


Copy the voice IDs

Open Voices, then My Voices. Click the three dots next to each voice you use and choose Copy
voice ID. Note which voice belongs to which speaker, plus the settings you use for each: model,
stability and similarity.

Hand it to Claude


     PROMPT · HAND OVER THE KEY

     Save this ElevenLabs API key and these voice IDs in a private settings file that only you
     read. Never put the key in a command, a log or a message.

     API key: [paste]
     [Speaker 1] voice ID: [paste]
     [Speaker 2] voice ID: [paste]
     Model: [for example eleven_v4]. Stability: [for example 0.5]. Similarity: [for example
     0.75].

     Whenever you make a voice file, also save its word timings (ElevenLabs returns them with
     the audio), so every cue can land on its word.

     Then test it: confirm both voices exist on my account, make one short line in each voice
     and send me the two files.


If both test files sound right, the setup is finished. From then on a full voiceover takes one sentence from you.

Two things can go wrong here. If Claude reports that it can't reach ElevenLabs, its workspace is
blocking the connection: open Settings, then Capabilities, turn on Allow network egress, wait a
minute and ask Claude to try again. And a key you paste into a chat stays in that chat's history,
which is why the restrictions and the credit limit matter. You can delete the key in ElevenLabs at any
time and create a new one with the same settings.

06 Why "Make Me a Cool Video" Gets a Template
Leave a blank, and AI fills it with its default. Ask for "a cool video" and every blank gets one: the
font, the colours, the way text appears, the pacing, the ending. Defaults are what AI-made videos
share, and that's why so many of them look the same.

A better tool won't fix this. Fewer blanks will.

It's the same idea behind all of The Exact Method: say exactly what you want, then check what you
get. For video, saying exactly what you want is the brief in section 07. Checking is the review in
section 10.




The Exact Method                                                                                        8
07 The Brief
Fill this in once per video. Send it together with the voice files, or put both in the project folder and
tell Claude where they are.


     TEMPLATE · THE BRIEF

     Goal and viewer. Who it's for, in one line: [for example, people who use ChatGPT for
     everyday decisions]. What they should do after watching: [for example, get the free PDF
     from the bio].

     Script. One line per voice file: [file name] · [speaker] · [the exact line]. Words that must
     appear on screen: [list them exactly, or write "your choice"].

     Look. Colours as hex codes: background [ ], text [ ], accent [ ], mistakes [ ]. Fonts, by
     name: headlines [ ], body [ ]. References: [two or three frames or videos, and what to take
     from each].

     Timing. Time everything to the real voice files, never to an estimate. Pauses: [for example
     0.3 s between lines, 0.5 s before the call to action]. Hold the last frame [about 1 s] after
     the last word.

     Never. [for example: nothing covers the presenter's hand; no text over busy screen text; no
     stretch of empty screen; no drop shadows, glows or bouncing text; nothing that appears in
     a single frame; "link in bio" never as plain text].

     Export. 1080x1920, 30 fps, voice at about -14 LUFS, peaks under -1 dB.


Each block closes a blank. The never-list does the most work: it's where your taste goes.

Each block exists because leaving it out causes a specific problem:

     Without an export block, a video can go out at 720x1280 with the voice at -20 LUFS, quieter
     than everything around it in the feed.

     Without a timing rule, captions drift. Half a second late is enough to feel wrong.
     Without a never-list, the AI places elements wherever there's space, including on top of a
     character's hand.



The Exact Method                                                                                            9
08 Prompt 1: From Brief to Storyboard

     PROMPT · BRIEF TO STORYBOARD

     Here's my brief. Before building anything, write a storyboard: one row per shot, with start
     and end times taken from the real lengths of the voice files; what's on screen and how it
     enters and leaves; and one sentence per shot on why it is there at that moment.

     Check every shot against the never-list. If anything in the brief is unclear, ask instead of
     guessing. Don't build anything until I approve the storyboard.


Read the "why" column first. A shot that can't say why it's there is usually filler.




The Exact Method                                                                                    10
09 Prompt 2: Build, Render, Check

     PROMPT · BUILD AND CHECK

     The storyboard is approved. Build the video in Remotion.

     Time every cue to the word it belongs to, using the word timings. Before the full render,
     render stills of the three busiest moments and check them against the never-list. Fix
     anything that fails, then render the full video.

     After the render, check it yourself before you show me: the timing against the voice at
     every cut; any frame that pops, flashes or goes black, including frame-by-frame sheets
     around every moment something appears; empty stretches, overlaps and cut-off text; the
     loudness and the resolution.

     Send me the video, a contact sheet of frames and a short list of what you checked and what
     you fixed.


Stills first saves time. A full render takes minutes, and a still shows most problems in seconds.

10 The Eight Checks, and the Fix Message
Watch every render on your phone, once with sound and once without, and check these eight things.

 CHECK                            WHAT TO LOOK FOR

 Timing                           Every caption and card lands on its word, not after it

 Dead time                        Any pause where nothing moves and nobody speaks

 Overlaps                         Text or cards on top of a face, a hand or other text

 Empty space                      Large areas with nothing in them, especially at the end

 The first three seconds          The hook works with the sound off

 Readability                      Small text that's fine on a laptop and unreadable on a phone

 Loudness                         About -14 LUFS. Quieter voices sound weak next to other videos

 Resolution                       1080x1920 at 30 fps



With the skills in section 13 installed, Claude runs most of these checks before you watch.
Automatic pop scans catch large jumps; a small element that appears in one frame (a strike-through,
an underline) can slip past them. That's what frame-by-frame sheets around each cue are for.


Prompt 3: the fix message

Vague feedback gets you a guess. Precise feedback gets you a fix.

 VAGUE                     PRECISE

 "Make it better."         "0:22: the card covers the presenter's hand. Move the cards up so the hand is clear."

 "The ending drags."       "The end card holds for about 7 s. Cut it to 1 s after the last word."

 "Fix the captions."       "From 0:19 the captions are about 0.5 s late. Time them to the word timestamps."



The pattern is the same every time: where it happens (the timestamp), what is wrong, and what to do
instead. Put every note in one message so Claude fixes them all in one pass.

11 Skills and Plugins, Explained
A skill is a folder of instructions Claude loads for one kind of job, such as building in Remotion,
avoiding certain visual habits or checking a render. A plugin bundles several skills, sometimes with
tools.

Without them, Claude falls back on its defaults, and that's where AI slop comes from. With them, it
follows rules someone's already tested on real videos.


     PROMPT · INSTALL A SKILL

     Install this skill from [GitHub link] into ~/.claude/skills/. Before you install anything, read
     its README and SKILL.md and tell me in one line whether anything looks unsafe or
     needs an API key. Install only the parts I name.


You can install part of a repository. Many contain several skills and you may need only one or two.

A skill is someone else's instructions, and often someone else's code. Read what it does before it
runs, and check the license: some free skills forbid commercial use.

13 The Anti-Slop Toolkit
"AI slop" is the look people now recognise in a second: shadows on every box, text that pops in,
glowing gradients, slides that never move. These skills exist to remove it. They work in different
ways, so they're worth combining.


no-slop-motion

github.com/ferndesk/no-slop-motion

A banned list, standing rules and a step-by-step approval process for animated videos. Its scripts
scan a render for pops, flashes and black frames, and make contact sheets of frames for review. The
rules work with any engine; its own build tools use HyperFrames (section 14).

Its default banned list, in plain words:

  1. Drop shadows on boxes and cards. Only buttons get depth.
  2. Glows, round colour gradients, dark frame edges and coloured "world" backgrounds.

  3. Grids of cards or lists of icons in place of an actual explanation.

  4. A screen that just sits there with nothing moving, like a slide.
  5. Text parked at the top of the frame while the action happens below.

  6. Anything that appears, vanishes or flashes within one or two frames, and cuts to black between
     shots.
  7. Glitch and shader transitions picked for "energy".

  8. Movement at a constant speed, and bouncy overshoot on interface elements.
  9. Colour filters that fake a look in place of chosen colours.

10. Customer quotes shown as tweet or chat screenshots.
 11. More than one call-to-action button in the brand colour in one frame.

12. A sound effect on every landing.

13. A voice stitched together from one-line clips, or cut in the middle of a word.

Impeccable

github.com/pbakaus/impeccable

The most widely used design-quality skill, with about 105,000 GitHub stars. Its slop detector applies
62 fixed rules to flag the usual AI design habits and needs no API key. It also has critique and audit
commands that rank what to fix first. It's built for interfaces, so it suits the look of scenes built from
web code (Remotion and HyperFrames scenes are), not timing or editing.


claude-motion-design

github.com/howseen-ai/claude-motion-design

Motion design in plain code (HTML, Playwright and ffmpeg) with no Remotion license. You
approve four stills before the full render, and a pop scan runs after it. About 790 stars. A security-
graded skills directory flags that it installs packages while it runs, so read what it installs first.


Claude Video 2.0

github.com/ItsssssJack/claude-video-2

You pick one of 21 visual styles from a gallery and answer five questions. It shows three stills for
approval, renders, then checks its own frames. MIT license. Optional AI clips use a paid Higgsfield
key.


video-use

github.com/browser-use/video-use

Made for raw footage, not animation. It cuts filler words and dead space, adds 30-millisecond audio
fades so cuts never click, and checks every cut in the render before showing you anything. MIT
license. It needs an ElevenLabs key, which it uses for transcription.

15 Reference Videos: Prompt Motion
The hardest part of the brief to put into words is the motion: how things enter, move and leave.
"Smooth" or "dynamic" leaves a blank, and Claude fills it with its default. A reference video closes
that blank.

prompt-motion.com is a free gallery of motion videos made with Claude, each shown with the
prompt or skill behind it. You can filter by type (Product UI, Phone, Charts, Kinetic type, Diagrams
and more), and every entry links to the person who made it.


How to borrow a reference

  1. Pick a video for its motion, not for its colours or its brand.

  2. Record your screen while it plays and send the recording to Claude. Claude studies a video
     through its frames, and the watch skill in section 12 helps.

  3. Send the prompt below.

  4. Keep the result as a reference video. Every later video that uses this animation starts from it, so
     your look stays the same from video to video.


     PROMPT · REBUILD A REFERENCE

     Here is a screen recording of a reference video. First, list every motion and feature in it, in
     order, with rough timings: how each element enters, moves and leaves, and every
     transition. Then rebuild it as a short reference video in my brief's colours, fonts and words.
     Copy the idea, the motion and the pace. Don't copy the colours, the shapes, the brand or
     the text. Check the remake against my never-list and replace anything on it.


Ask for the list first and check it. Anything missing from the list will be missing from the remake.

Two cautions. Videos and prompts belong to their creators, so borrow the motion, leave the rest of
their work alone, and credit the creator if you share their prompt. And popular entries often use
habits from the banned list in section 13, such as letter-by-letter typing or glows. Your never-list
decides what stays.

16 AI Footage Inside Claude: Higgsfield
Remotion draws everything with code: text, shapes, layouts, screens. It can't film a person, a product
on a table or a street at night. Higgsfield can generate that. It's a paid platform with many image and
video models (Kling, Seedance, Veo and Nano Banana among them), and its connector lets Claude
use them from the chat: Claude picks a model, sets it up, makes the clip and shows it to you.

Use it for the shots code can't draw, and keep code for everything else: text, captions, timing and the
end card. The clip goes into the Remotion project like any other video file.


Connect it

  1. In Claude, open Settings, then Connectors, and click Add custom connector.

  2. Name it Higgsfield and paste this address: https://mcp.higgsfield.ai/mcp

  3. Click Connect, sign in to Higgsfield and approve access.

  4. In Claude Code, run these three commands instead: npm i -g @higgsfield/cli, then higgsfield
     auth login, then npx skills add higgsfield-ai/skills.


What it costs

You need an active paid Higgsfield plan. Every clip Claude makes spends credits. The unlimited
offers on Higgsfield's website don't apply to clips made through Claude. As of October 2026, plans
start at $9 a month for 120 credits. A 5-second Kling 3.0 clip costs about 8 credits, and a 4-second
Veo 3.1 clip about 40. Prices changed several times in 2026, so check the pricing page before you
pick a plan.

There's no hard spending limit for Claude. You can name a limit in your prompt, but Claude can still
go over it, so the prompt on the next page makes Claude ask before it spends anything.

Ask before it spends


     PROMPT · ONE AI CLIP

     Using Higgsfield, make one [5]-second vertical clip: [the shot: subject, setting, camera,
     light]. Before you make anything, tell me which model you'll use, the settings and the credit
     cost, and wait for my yes. Stay under [N] credits in this chat. Then add the clip to the video
     at [timestamp] and run the eight checks again.


Describe the shot like a camera operator would: what's in frame, where the camera is, how it moves, what the
light is like.



Before you post a clip

     License. The free plan has restricted commercial rights. Check Higgsfield's terms for your plan
     before you use a clip in a video that sells something.

     Files. Claude can't pass files from the chat to Higgsfield. Upload reference images in
     Higgsfield, or give Claude a direct link.

     Checks. AI footage gets the same eight checks as everything else, plus one more: look for
     warped hands, faces and lettering in every clip.

     Labels. Platforms have rules for labelling AI-made footage. Check the rule where you post.

17 What This Won't Do
It won't give you taste. Claude follows the brief and the rules; whether the result is good is your call.

It can't catch what it doesn't check, so ask for the contact sheet and the check list with every render.

Skills are other people's work. Read them before you install them and check their licenses. The same
goes for reference videos and their prompts.

A reference video or an AI clip won't save a weak script.

A precise brief makes a better video. It doesn't guarantee views.
